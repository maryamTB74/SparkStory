---
title: Read-aloud techniques in nursery rhymes
source: Mother Goose and traditional English nursery rhymes (Project Gutenberg)
licence: public domain
---

Refrain: one line comes back unchanged every time round. "Hickory dickory dock" opens
the rhyme and closes it, so a listening child can join in on the line they already
know.

---

Bookending: the story ends on the same words it began with, changed by everything in
between. The last page earns its echo.

---

Three attempts: the character tries three times, the first two failing and the third
working. Three is the smallest number that makes a pattern and then breaks it.

---

Cumulative repetition: each verse repeats everything before it and adds one thing, as
in "This is the house that Jack built". The pleasure is in the growing list, and the
child remembers it before the reader does.

---

Call and response: one voice asks and another answers, as in "Pussycat, pussycat,
where have you been?" It gives a read-aloud two parts to play.

---

Sound-play: words chosen for how they feel in the mouth rather than what they mean --
"hickory dickory", "hey diddle diddle". Nonsense syllables are legitimate, and young
children prefer them.

---

Alliteration: several words close together starting on the same sound, so the line
almost pulls itself along. "Peter Piper picked a peck."

---

A turning line: a single short line that changes everything, set apart from the
longer lines around it. Brevity is what makes it land.

---

Counting or naming in threes: a small list of concrete things, never more than three
or four, so the ear can hold them all.

---

The page-turn question: the page stops just before the answer, so turning it is part
of the story. Used sparingly -- if every page asks, the pattern becomes a tic and
stops working.

---

A rhythm a reader can find without being told: the same number of beats in each line,
so an adult reading it cold falls into the pattern by the second page.

---

Naming the same thing the same way each time. A child learning a story wants "the red
boots" to stay "the red boots", not become "her footwear".
